About the book

Part memoir, part notebook of ideas, Nkuru Yogeye follows a boy from Bwiyando through school, the Minor Seminary of Nkumba, Nyamugali and university life in Kigali. It keeps the humour of childhood alongside the difficult parts, without pretending that every memory is neat or every question has an easy answer.

The later pages turn from events to meaning: what curiosity taught him, how art and language shape a mind, what engineering might contribute to Rwanda, and what kind of person he hopes to become. Poetry, letters, family rituals, faith, love, debate and technical ambition share the same pages.

Part I · The Hills Where I Began

The Boy Who Arrived in Bwiyando

“Every great journey starts somewhere. Mine started in a village the map had not yet bothered to learn.”

On 10 July 2006, at about eleven in the morning, a baby named Niyonkuru Donatien arrived in Bwiyando Village, Nyacina Cell, Gashenyi Sector, Gakenke District, Rwanda. Nobody threw a parade. The hills stayed where they were, the cows kept chewing, and my mother, I am told, was mostly interested in getting some rest.

I will say it plainly: Bwiyando is not only where I was born. It is where I grew up, and almost everything I later wrote in my notebook has red soil on its boots.

Part II · Goats, Pencils and Big Questions

The Little Businessman of Bwiyando

“The first business lesson is rarely taught in a classroom. It begins the day someone pays you less than you expected.”

Near the end of 2016, during the holidays, I started selling small things that I found or collected. Looking back, I call it the beginning of my trading career. I gave it a name, RIT-I-SAH, my own playful phrase joining matches and a clock. Every company needs a name before it needs money. I had followed that rule by accident.

Part III · The Seminary Years

Why I Turned Away from Priesthood

“A sign on the door says what a house wants to be. The way it treats the sick says what it is.”

I went to the seminary because the road to it looked like the road to something important. Priesthood, as I understood it then, meant service, discipline, belonging and a life turned toward God. I admired it. I also watched it closely, and watching closely is a habit that does not come with an off switch.

Part IV · Nyamugali: Many Things at Once

The Broken Piano and the New Face of Mathematics

“A broken piano taught me more physics than a working one ever could, mostly about my own ignorance.”

ES Nyamugali had a piano. I loved music, and I loved being near that piano, and then it was destroyed. I tried to repair it, and I could not. That sentence is short because the feeling was long.

A piano is a meeting point of art and engineering. When it fails, a musician sees a lost instrument while a stubborn student sees a question: what failed, how could I find out, and what would I need to know to bring it back? When I later chose Electronics and Telecommunication Engineering, the piano was somewhere in the room.

Part V · Kigali, Code and the Present

Code, Debate and the Door to IEEE

“The classroom teaches one language at a time. Curiosity keeps learning the rest.”

As I settled into university, I kept looking for ways to connect my interests. I began to learn C and C++, then Java and Python, and later I met Assembly. I wanted to understand how software and computers work, and how that knowledge could connect with the projects I imagined.

Debate stayed part of my life. I became a judge, which meant I could keep developing the public-speaking and critical-thinking skills I had practised before university.

Part VI · What the Stories Taught Me

Technology for Rwanda, Rooted in Place

“Africa can borrow the world’s tools and still design its own furniture.”

My long-term ambition is to build technology in Rwanda that values our cultural and environmental context. I do not want innovation to mean importing a design without asking whether it fits the people, climate, languages, resources, and daily realities around it.

A farmer should not have to become a software engineer to operate a useful irrigation system. A device should respect the work it is meant to support.

Part VII · Letters, Rules and Promises

Keep Becoming

“Plans are maps. You are the person walking.”

You do not need to solve your whole life today. You need to make the next honest decision.

If you have energy, do the work. If you are confused, ask a clearer question. If you are wrong, correct it. If you are tired, rest. If a project is too large, reduce its scope.

Rooted in place.
